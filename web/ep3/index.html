Strange Attractors

1Three equations, one moving point

$$\dot x = \sigma(y-x),\qquad \dot y = x(\rho - z) - y,\qquad \dot z = xy - \beta z.$$ They describe Lorenz's convection roll, and exactly a leaky water wheel: $x$ is how fast the wheel turns, $y$ how lopsided it is, $z$ how top-heavy. The divergence of the velocity field is $-(\sigma + 1 + \beta)$, a negative constant: every blob of initial conditions loses volume at the same rate, so the motion collapses onto a set of zero volume, the attractor.

2Turning up the heat

With $\sigma = 10$, $\beta = 8/3$, the story as $\rho$ increases (use the presets above):

  • $\rho < 1$: everything falls to rest at the origin.
  • $\rho = 1$: rest becomes unstable; two steady rolls $C_\pm = (\pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho-1)}, \pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho-1)}, \rho-1)$ take over.
  • $13.93 < \rho < 24.06$: transient chaos. Orbits wander chaotically, sometimes for long, then settle on a roll.
  • $\rho_H = \sigma(\sigma+\beta+3)/(\sigma-\beta-1) \approx 24.74$: the rolls lose stability (Hopf). Between 24.06 and 24.74, rolls and chaos coexist.
  • $\rho = 28$: the strange attractor. Near $\rho \approx 100$: periodic orbits again, and a period-doubling cascade (§5).

3The butterfly, measured

Two starting points $10^{-k}$ apart. On a log scale their distance grows along a straight line of slope $\lambda_1 \approx 0.906$ (the largest Lyapunov exponent) until it saturates at the size of the attractor. So each factor 10 of extra precision buys only $\ln 10/\lambda_1 \approx 2.5$ time units of prediction.

Lyapunov spectrum at $\rho = 28$: . The sum equals the divergence $-(\sigma+1+\beta) = -13.667$: stretching, neutral, crushing.

4Stretch and fold: a drop of ink

Chaos in a bounded region needs two things: stretching (sensitivity) and folding (to stay bounded). Part 1's map $4x(1-x)$ does both to an interval; the Lorenz flow does both to a sheet. Release a drop of 20 000 initial conditions and watch it. The resulting object is a stack of infinitely many sheets, with Kaplan–Yorke dimension $D = 2 + \lambda_1/|\lambda_3| \approx 2.06$.

5Part 1, hidden inside

Lorenz (1963) recorded the successive maxima $z_1, z_2, \dots$ of $z(t)$ and plotted $z_{n+1}$ against $z_n$. The points fall on a thin curve with one hump: a one-dimensional map, which we can cobweb exactly as in Part 1. Its slope exceeds 1 in absolute value everywhere, so no periodic orbit can be stable. Change $\rho$ with the slider in §1.

Because the flow hides a one-hump map, Feigenbaum's universality applies. Near $\rho \approx 100$ the stable orbit doubles its period as $\rho$ decreases, and the gaps between doublings shrink by a factor tending to $\delta = 4.669\ldots$ Click the diagram to load that $\rho$ in the 3-D view.

6The skeleton: unstable periodic orbits

The attractor is threaded with infinitely many periodic orbits, all unstable. Each is named by its itinerary: L for a loop around the left roll, R for the right. A chaotic orbit shadows one, is ejected (by the multiplier μ per lap), shadows another. Averages over the attractor can be computed from these cycles alone (periodic-orbit theory). Computed here by Newton's method on the Poincaré section $z = 27$.
