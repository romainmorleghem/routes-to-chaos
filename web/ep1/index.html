Routes to Chaos · Part 1

The Logistic Map & Feigenbaum's Universal Constant

A one-line population model contains period doubling, chaos, and a number that shows up in fluids, circuits and lasers. Every figure on this page is live: move the sliders, zoom, click.

1A very simple rule

Let $x_n \in [0,1]$ be the size of a population in year $n$, as a fraction of the maximum the environment can sustain. Each year it is multiplied by a fertility $r$ (growth), but the closer it gets to 1 the less food there is (crowding):

$$x_{n+1} = f(x_n) = r\,x_n\,(1-x_n).$$

To iterate graphically, go vertically to the curve (that's $x_{n+1}$), then horizontally to the diagonal $y=x$ — the diagonal turns the output back into an input — and repeat. The staircase is a cobweb diagram; on the right, the same values against time. For $r<3$ every orbit ends on the fixed point $x^\ast = 1 - 1/r$, where the curve crosses the diagonal.

2When a fixed point breaks

Why does the orbit converge? Near $x^\ast$ the curve is almost a straight line of slope $f'(x^\ast)$, so the distance to the fixed point is multiplied by that slope at every step:

$$x_{n+1}-x^\ast \approx f'(x^\ast)\,(x_n - x^\ast), \qquad f'(x^\ast) = r(1-2x^\ast) = 2-r .$$

If $|f'(x^\ast)|<1$ the distance shrinks (stable); a negative slope makes the orbit zig-zag around $x^\ast$. At $r=3$ the slope reaches $-1$ and the fixed point becomes unstable. The orbit then settles on a 2-cycle $a\to b\to a$. Since $f(f(a))=a$, the cycle points are fixed points of $f\circ f$: tick the box to see them. Notice that, around $x=\tfrac12$, $f\circ f$ looks like a small upside-down copy of $f$. The same argument applied to $f\circ f$ predicts the next doubling at $r=1+\sqrt6\approx 3.449$.

3The period-doubling cascade

Forget the transient and plot, above each $r$, the values the orbit keeps returning to. This is the bifurcation diagram. Click anywhere on it to see the corresponding orbit; scroll to zoom, drag to move, double-click to reset.

The forks come faster and faster. With $\Delta_n = r_{n+1}-r_n$, the ratio of successive gaps converges (values below computed with 60-digit arithmetic):

$\displaystyle \delta = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Delta_{n-1}}{\Delta_n} = 4.669\,201\,609\,102\,990\ldots$ — Feigenbaum's constant (1975). Because the gaps shrink geometrically their sum is finite, and infinitely many doublings accumulate before $r_\infty = 3.569\,945\,671\,8\ldots$

4Beyond $r_\infty$: chaos

Beyond $r_\infty$ the orbit never repeats, but it is still perfectly deterministic. Start two orbits $10^{-k}$ apart: the gap $\varepsilon_n$ grows exponentially, $\varepsilon_n \approx \varepsilon_0 e^{\lambda n}$ (a straight line on the log plot), until it is as large as the signal itself.

Why exponential? Each step multiplies a small error by $|f'(x_n)|$ — the same slope as in chapter 2. The average growth rate is the Lyapunov exponent $$\lambda = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\ln|f'(x_k)| .$$ $\lambda<0$: stable cycle (green); $\lambda>0$: chaos (red). Inside the chaos, windows of order reappear — the widest starts at $r = 1+\sqrt8$ with period 3. Zoom into it: a miniature copy of the whole diagram, with the same $\delta$.

5Universality

Change the rule. Any map with a single smooth hump goes through the same cascade, with the same $\delta$. Only the shape of the very top of the hump matters: a flat (quartic) top belongs to a different universality class.

The same constant has been measured in experiments — fluid layers heated from below, nonlinear electronic circuits, lasers — where nobody wrote down a parabola.

6Why? Renormalisation

At the special parameters $R_n$ (where $x=\tfrac12$ lies on the $2^n$-cycle), look at $f^{2^n}$ near $x=\tfrac12$ inside a box of size $|\alpha|^{-n}$. Blown up by $\alpha^n$ (with $\alpha=-2.5029\ldots$, the minus sign meaning a 180° rotation), it reproduces the previous stage — better and better as $n$ grows. The rescaled curves converge to a universal function $g$, the fixed point of the doubling operator

$$T[f](x) = \alpha\, f\big(f(x/\alpha)\big), \qquad T[g] = g .$$

In the space of all one-hump maps, $T$ pulls maps toward $g$ along a whole surface, and pushes them away along a single direction, multiplying distances by $\delta$. Each period doubling is one application of $T$, so the gaps between the $R_n$ shrink by $\delta$ — for any family of maps that crosses that surface. $\delta$ and $\alpha$ are properties of period doubling itself.

·Epilogue: into the complex plane

With $z = r(\tfrac12 - x)$ the logistic map becomes $z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c$ with $c = r/2 - r^2/4$. Top: the bifurcation diagram in $c$ (so $r$ grows to the left). Bottom: let $c$ be complex — the Mandelbrot set. Each fork sits where two bulbs touch; the period-3 window is a small copy of the whole set. That is the subject of part 2.
